Multi-agent lead-response assistant for a car dealership

At Effective Business Automations, I rebuilt the AI that answered a car dealership's online leads by text. The original was one agent with one big prompt, and it sometimes made things up. I replaced it with a director agent that routes each question to a specialist working from the real inventory, plus a handoff to staff when it doesn't know.

The setup

The dealership (a local used-car lot) lists its cars on CarGurus, Edmunds, Cars.com and similar sites. When a shopper clicks "I'm interested" on one of those sites, the lead (name, phone, email, which car) lands in the dealer's inbox. The goal was simple: reply to every lead right away, at any hour.

So we automated the first response. A parser watched the dealer's inbox and pulled out each lead. Right away, the shopper got a text with a link to that car's full info packet and a short note: "Saw you were interested in this car, let us know if you have any questions." Any reply was answered by an AI agent. The pipeline ran on Make and Zapier, with OpenAI models doing the talking.

The problem: one agent doing everything

The first version, built before I joined, was a single agent with one long prompt. It had to work out what the shopper wanted, know the inventory, know the dealership, and write the reply, all at once. With that much on its plate, it filled gaps by guessing.

The clearest example: a shopper asked if we could send a video of the car. He thought he was texting a salesperson. The bot replied with an enthusiastic yes. It had no way to record or send a video, so the video never came, and a real customer was left waiting on a promise the system couldn't keep.

What I built: a director and specialists

My idea was to stop asking one agent to be good at everything. Every incoming text first goes to a director agent. Its only job is to work out what the shopper is asking and route it. It never answers anyone itself.

These were the main specialists. There were others for other kinds of questions, all built the same way.

  • Inventory agent. Works out which car and what detail the shopper wants, looks it up in the inventory database, and passes the facts to the reply writer. Price, mileage and features come from data, never from the model's memory.
  • Dealership agent. Answers questions about the dealership itself, like hours and location.
  • Reply writer. Turns the facts into a short, friendly text that sounds like the dealership.
  • Honest fallback. If no specialist can answer, the shopper gets "We don't have the answer to that one. Check our website or call us at…" instead of a made-up reply. People asked strange things, and this is what kept the weird ones safe.

The trade-off

Routing through several agents made each reply a little slower than one agent answering directly. I took that trade on purpose. Each agent's prompt was small enough to get right and easy to test on its own, and a reply that's a few seconds slower but true beats an instant one that loses a sale.

Result

Answers became either accurate, pulled from real inventory and dealership info, or an honest handoff to a person. The made-up promises stopped. There were still some bugs to chase, and we never set up formal accuracy tracking, so I don't have a percentage to quote. The change in what customers were told was obvious, though.

What I took from it: an agent that's sure of itself is only useful if it's right. Small agents with one job, real data instead of memory, and a clear "I don't know" path made the system something a business could trust with its customers.

My role

I joined the agency after the first version was live. The multi-agent redesign (the director, the specialist split, and the fallback) was my idea, and I built it. I also helped on the agency's other automations, like post-purchase texts triggered from a store's point-of-sale system and automatic review requests after a visit.
